## 1. SOĞUK AÇILIŞ (0:00–0:50)
🇹🇷 Test videosundaki açılış + "köpekbalığı gibi değil, sürü halinde geliyor" fikri + açık soru: *neden kırmızıya dönüyorlar?*

> Sea of Cortez. Midnight.
> You're twelve metres down, and your torch is the only light in the water.
> Then the beam catches something.
> One shape. Then ten. Then too many to count.
> And every single one of them... is turning red.
> The fishermen here have a name for them. Diablo rojo. The red devil.
>
> Now, when you picture a dangerous animal in the ocean, you probably picture a shark. One big predator, one set of teeth. This is different. A Humboldt squid isn't a giant. The biggest are about the length of an adult human. But it almost never shows up alone. It hunts in the dark, in groups that can number in the hundreds. And when it gets excited, its whole body starts flashing like a warning light.
>
> So why do they turn red? Hold that thought. First, let's open the file.

## 2. OLAY DOSYASI (0:50–1:50)
🇹🇷 Kim, nerede, ne büyüklükte. Gündüz oksijensiz derinlikte bekler, gece yüzeye çıkar — tam dalgıcın olduğu yere.

> Encounter File number one. *Dosidicus gigas*. The Humboldt squid, sometimes called the jumbo squid.
>
> It lives in the eastern Pacific, from Chile all the way up to Mexico, and lately, much further north. A big one can have a body about one and a half metres long and weigh around fifty kilograms. That's roughly the weight of a large dog, packed into a torpedo of pure muscle.
>
> During the day, it's nowhere near you. It sits hundreds of metres down, in water so low in oxygen that most fish can't survive there for long. The squid can. It slows itself down and waits.
>
> Then the sun sets. And the whole population rises. Every night, they travel up toward the surface to feed, and by midnight they can be right where a night diver would be. Like, say... you.

🎬 Dosya kartı (daktilo) → Pasifik haritası, yayılım alanı boyanır → kalamar + büyük köpek silueti terazi/ölçek → derinlik kesiti: gündüz/gece animasyonu (güneş batar, sürü yükselir, derinlik sayacı) → dalgıç "YOU" işaretiyle belirir.

## 3. SİLAHLARI (1:50–3:10)
🇹🇷 Hız (jet itiş), dişli vantuzlar, papağan gagası — ve bunların yüzlercesi birlikte avlanıyor olabilir.

> So what happens when they get there? Let's go through the equipment.
>
> First, speed. A Humboldt squid swims by jet propulsion. It sucks water into its body and blasts it out through a tube called a siphon. That can push it to over twenty kilometres an hour in short bursts. A good swimmer in fins manages maybe three or four. You are not winning a race here.
>
> Second, the arms. Eight arms and two longer tentacles, all covered in suckers. And these aren't the soft, rubbery suckers you'd find on an octopus. Each one is ringed with tiny, sharp teeth. Once they close on something, they don't slide off. They dig in.
>
> Third, the beak. Right in the middle of all those arms sits a hard, hooked beak, a lot like a parrot's. It's built to tear flesh into pieces small enough to swallow.
>
> So: speed, grip, and a blade. Now multiply it by a few hundred.
>
> These squid don't just happen to be in the same place. Sonar studies suggest they may actually coordinate when they hunt at night. Which brings us back to that question.

🎬 Jet itiş kesit animasyonu (su girer/çıkar) → hız yarışı çubukları (squid vs dalgıç) → vantuz yakın çekim, diş halkası zoom → gaga açılıp kapanır → "×300" sayaç, kalamar çoğalır → sonar ekranında koordineli hareket eden noktalar.

## 4. NEDEN KIRMIZI? (3:10–4:20) — açık döngünün cevabı
🇹🇷 Bilim insanları kalamarlara kamera taktı. Sadece kırmızı renk hücreleri var; saniyede birkaç kez kırmızı↔beyaz yanıp sönüyorlar, neredeyse hep başka kalamar görünce. Muhtemelen birbirleriyle "konuşuyorlar".

> Why do they turn red?
>
> To find out, scientists did something slightly unhinged. They strapped cameras onto live Humboldt squid and released them back into the sea. A squid-cam.
>
> What they saw was this. The squid's skin is packed with tiny colour cells, and in this species, they come in just one colour: red. Expand them, and the squid goes deep red. Squeeze them shut, and it turns pale white. And it can switch between the two several times a second.
>
> Here's the interesting part. The flashing almost always happened when other squid were in view. The researchers think it's a signal, a way for squid to talk to each other in the dark. They can even change the speed and the strength of the flashing.
>
> So when the water around you turns into a field of flashing red, you're not looking at a warning. You're looking at a conversation. And you have absolutely no idea what's being said. Which is somehow worse.

🎬 Kalamara kamera bağlanır (komik an) → "SQUID-CAM" kayıt ekranı POV → deri yakın çekim: renk hücreleri genişler/daralır → kırmızı/beyaz flaş, 3 Hz metronom → iki kalamar karşılıklı flaş, aralarında sinyal dalgası → dalgıç ortada, "?" baloncukları.

## 5. GERÇEK KARŞILAŞMALAR (4:20–6:30)
🇹🇷 Önce efsane (denize düşen balıkçılar) → kanıt yok, efsane olarak işaretlenir. Sonra gerçek anlatımlar: Scott Cassell'in kendi ifadeleri; 2009 San Diego/La Jolla akını. Biliminsanı: "insan yiyici değiller, çoğu merak."

> Okay. So they're fast, they grab, and they talk. But has any of this actually been used against people?
>
> Here's where we need to be careful, because this animal attracts a lot of tall tales.
>
> Let's start with the legend. Fishermen in the Sea of Cortez have long told stories of men who fell overboard at night and were never found, and they blame the red devils. Those stories are old and widely repeated. But we found no confirmed case of a Humboldt squid killing a human. So that one goes in the legend pile.
>
> Now, the real accounts.
>
> Scott Cassell is a diver and filmmaker who has spent years diving with Humboldt squid in Mexico. And by his own account, it hasn't always gone well. He has said a squid once dragged him down so fast that his eardrum ruptured. He's described having his arm pulled out of its socket. And another squid, he says, tore into his scalp. Today, he dives with them wearing a custom suit of body armour.
>
> And it's not just one diver. In the summer of 2009, thousands of jumbo squid suddenly showed up in shallow water off San Diego, near La Jolla, California. Divers reported squid grabbing at their masks, their cameras, and their gear, and some decided to stay out of the water altogether.
>
> But listen to how the scientists described it. One biologist pointed out that these squid aren't the man-eaters they'd been made out to be. A lot of the grabbing looked more like curiosity than attack.
>
> Picture an animal that explores the world with arms full of teeth. Curiosity still leaves marks.

🎬 "LEGEND / CONFIRMED" iki sütunlu dosya; efsane kartına "UNVERIFIED" damgası → ENCOUNTER kartı: dalgıç + zırh silueti, derinlik sayacı hızla düşer, kulak ikonunda kırmızı flaş, kol/kafa yaralanma işaretleri → 2009 haritası: San Diego'ya akın eden noktalar, tarih damgası → dalgıç maskesine uzanan kollar → bilim insanı alıntı kartı. `[KANIT]` uygun stok sualtı/kalamar görüntüsü bulunursa 2 sn.

## 6. YOUR MOVE (6:30–7:50)
🇹🇷 4 seçenek ve her birinin sorunu; sonunda deneyimli dalgıçların gerçekte ne yaptığı.

> So let's put you back in the water. Midnight. Twelve metres down. Red flashes all around you. Your move.
>
> Option one. Swim for the surface. The problem: they're several times faster than you. And shooting straight up from depth is one of the most dangerous things a diver can do. A rapid ascent can seriously injure your lungs. You'd be escaping one danger by swimming into another.
>
> Option two. Stay perfectly still. Honestly, better. But remember, a Humboldt squid investigates with its arms. Staying still might just make you a more interesting thing to touch.
>
> Option three. Turn off your torch. This one's tempting. And here's a strange fact: squid boats in this part of the world use bright lights to draw squid in at night. Light really does bring them closer. But switch it off, and now you're blind, in the dark, surrounded by animals with huge eyes built for exactly this. They can see you. You can't see them.
>
> Option four. Fight back. With what? Ten arms, hundreds of teeth, and a few hundred friends.
>
> So what do people who dive with them actually do? They plan for it. They dive with protection, stay calm, stay together, and avoid the frenzy around injured or hooked squid, because that's when things get chaotic. In other words, the real answer is to never end up alone in that water without a plan.

🎬 Test videosundaki YOUR MOVE paneli; her seçenek seçilince kısa animasyon: (1) yukarı ok + akciğer ikonunda uyarı, (2) kollar dalgıcı yoklar, (3) fener söner → kalamar gözleri karanlıkta parlar, tekne ışıkları üstten, (4) sayaç "10 ARMS · 300+ TEETH · ×300" → "WHAT DIVERS ACTUALLY DO" kontrol listesi.

## 7. TERS KÖŞE (7:50–9:20)
🇹🇷 Yamyamlar; ömürleri ~1 yıl; okyanus değiştikçe Alaska'ya kadar yayılıyorlar. Asıl ters köşe: Peru tek başına 2014'te 500 bin tonun üzerinde yakaladı — restorandaki kalamar büyük ihtimalle bu tür. "Asıl şeytan kim?"

> And speaking of injured squid, here's something that might change how you see all this.
>
> Humboldt squid are cannibals. When one gets hurt, say, caught on a fishing line, the others will often turn on it. Your fellow red devil is also on the menu.
>
> They also live fast. Most Humboldt squid only live around a year. Two, if they're lucky. Everything you've seen, the speed, the hunting, the flashing, happens in one short, intense life.
>
> And they're doing something few animals manage right now. They're expanding. Over the past couple of decades, they've pushed north from Mexico along the coast, all the way to Alaska. One leading idea is that low-oxygen zones in the ocean are spreading, and that's exactly the kind of water this squid can handle and many of its competitors can't. The ocean is changing, and this animal seems to be winning.
>
> But here's the twist. For all the stories about the red devil attacking people, the numbers go overwhelmingly the other way. Humboldt squid support one of the biggest squid fisheries on the planet. In 2014, Peru alone landed more than half a million tonnes of them. Even if every single squid were a fifty-kilo giant, that's more than ten million animals in one year, from one country.
>
> If you've ever eaten a squid steak or a plate of calamari, there's a real chance it came from this animal.
>
> So... who's really the devil here?

🎬 Oltaya takılan kalamara diğerleri döner → yaşam çizgisi (0→12 ay, hızla dolan çubuk) → Kuzey Amerika kıyısı: kırmızı alan Meksika'dan Alaska'ya yayılır, oksijen haritası soluklaşır → ışıklı balıkçı filosu (test videosundaki tekne) çoğalır → sayaç 556.000 TON → "10.000.000+" → kalamar halkası tabağı → ekranda "WHO'S THE DEVIL?" ve kamera dalgıca değil tekneye döner.
